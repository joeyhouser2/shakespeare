Gyve wit and woful voice, O Sire, let me my woes complain.
For this the moon hath hid her face, and thrice come back again.
Sith first I set me down, in slouth and sobbing cheare
No muse to comfort care.
What man can make a spring of tears, to feed my gulf of grief
Or who hath store of tears so great: and far from all relief.
Pity hath bereft my sight, and hart hath cleft in twayne,
Which suffereth not my woes to sound, my tong cannot complain
Such is my grief.
Loe now my study stayed is, for cruel death have slain.
My brother deare: shall I poor wretch in wretched life remain?
The only hope of all our house, O death thou hast bereft me
Mine earthly joy this brother was, none other joy is left me.
My country wrought my woe, my friends did hate me all,
If death had tane my guiltless soul, no grief had made me thrall.
Lo yet among you men I live, and still enjoy this light
But long I may not so.
Your deadly darts (O foes) for pity cast in me:
With cruel sword before the rest, let me destroyed be.
Else thou Almighty God, on me such mercy have,
As that my wretched head may rest, within mine earthly grave.
But him a careful cloud, did compass round about,
And on his head with heavy hand, the dust he poured out.
And in my greenest years, when youth hath highest power,
Shall this my spirit depart away, and death my corpse devour?
The Gods I cannot guide, their will we must obay,
Where destiny dryves I yield my self: with willing mind alway.
Is not our sleep (O fool) of death, an Image plain?
For fatal course shall bring a rest, that ever will remain.
Now eat, now drink, now make good sport:
For sith thy felfe on earth, a mortal man do see
Take here thy fill of earthly joys, no joys hereafter be.
In fortunes high disgrace: each man may death disdain.
But he most valiant is, that can, in wretched state remain.
And as twice twentye years bypast, so now my force I find
Mine aged years are worn away, I feel my youthful mind.
For either soon or late, in order as men say,
The wretched flock of wordly folk, to death do take their way.

Thou dastard knight, Damatrion, thy mother here have slain.
That doest both her, and Spartayn blood: with cowerdice distain
Although unweldyage, when life doth wear away,
None other ill did bring withal, but that, (as men do say)
By living long full oft we see: wich we would not behold.
It seemeth well that cowerds weep, when they be brought to grave
But thou my son a Spartayn true, no weeping tears shalt have.
