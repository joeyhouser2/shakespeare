The Partridge in the pretty Merlines foot,
Who feels her force supprest with fearfulness,
And finds that strength nor strife can do her boot,
To scape the danger of her deep distress:
These woeful words may seem for to rehearse
Which I must write in this waimenting verse.

What helpeth now (sayeth she) dame natures skill,
To die my feathers like the dusty ground?
Or what prevails to lend me wings at will
Which in the air can make my body bound?
Since from the earth the dogs me drave perforce,
And now aloft the Hauke hath caught my corse.

If change of coollors, could not me convey,
Yet mought my wings have scapt the dogs despite:
And if my wings did fail to fly away,
Yet m•ught my strength resist the Merlynes might.
But nature made the Merlyne me to kill,
And me to yield unto the Merlines will.

My lot is like (deer Dame) beleve me well,
The quiet life which I full closely kept:
Was not content in happy state to dwell,
But forth in hast to gaze on thee it leapt.
Desire the dog did spring me up in hast,
Thou wert the Hauke, whose talents caught me fast.

What should I then, seek means to fly away?
Or strive by force, to break out of thy feet?
No, no, perdy, I may no strength assay,
To strive with thee ywis, it were not meet.
Thou art that Hauke, whom nature made to hent me,
And I the Bird, that must therewith content me.

And since Dame nature hath ordained so,
Her happy heast I gladly shall embrace:
I yield my will, although it were to woe,
I stand content to take, my grief for grace:
And seal it up within my secret hart,
Which seal receive, as token of my smart.
