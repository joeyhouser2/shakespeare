Faiths younger sister that Speranza hight,
Was clad in blew, that her beseemed well:
Not all so cheerful seemed she of sight
As was her sister: whether dread did dwell
Or anguish in her heart, is hard to tell:
Upon her arm a silver anchor lay,
Whereon she leaned ever as befell.
And ever up to heaven as she did pray,
Her eyes were bent, ne swerved other way.
—Hope a handsome maid,
Of cheerful look and lovely to behold:
In silken Samite she was light araide,
And her fair locks were woven up in gold:
She alway smiled, and in her hand did hold
An holy water sprinkle, dipt in dew,
With which she sprinkled favours manifold.
On whom she list, and did great liking shewe,
Great liking unto many, but true love to few.
True hope is swift, and flies with swallows wings,
Kings it makes Gods, and meaner creatures Kings.
Wan Hope poor soul, on broken anchor sits
Wringing his arms, as robbed of his bliss.
What better emperor can the body hold,
Then sacred Hope? the element from whence,
Vertue is drawn fresh looking, never old:
Matter most worthy of a strong defence.
It animates yoong men, and makes them bold,
Arming their hearts with holy influence,
It like a seal in tender thoughts doth press
The perfect Image of all happiness.
— Hope is double, and hath double power,
As being mortal, and immortal fram'de:
In th'one she's moveless, certain every hour:
In th'other doubtful, and incertain nam'de.
Th'immortall Hope raines in a holy bower,
In earthy closures is the mortal tam'de.
And these two contraries, where ere they meet,
Double delight, and make our thoughts more sweet.
He that hopes least, leaves not to hope at all,
But hopes the most, hoping so little hope,
Augmenting of our hope, makes hope grow small,
And taking from it, gives it greater scope.
The desperate man which in despair doth fall,
Hopes by that end ill fortune to revoke,
And to this hope belongs a second part,
Which we call confidence, which rules the hart.
This second part of hope, this confidence,
Doth Tully call a vertue that doth guide
The Spirit to an honest residence,
Without whose aid, no pleasure will abide
In our world-wearied flesh.
All men are guests where hope doth hold the feast.
Such is the weakness of all mortal hope,
So tickle is the state of earthly things,
And brings us bale and bitter sorrowings,
That ere they come unto their aimed scope,
They fall too short of our frail reckonings,
In stead of comfort which we should embrace.
This is the death of Keysars and of Kings,
Let none therefore that is in meaner place,
Too greatly grieve at any unlucky case.
Unworthy they of grace, whom one denial
Excludes from fairest hope, without farther trial.
Hope like the Hyenna coming to be old,
Alters his shape, is turned to despair.
Sorrow doth utter what us still doth grieve,
But hope forbids us sorrow to beleeve.
— Our hopes good deceives us,
But that we would forgo that seldom leaves us.
None without great hopes will follow such,
Whose power and honour doth not promise much.
Who nothing hopes, let him despair in nought.
To live in hope of that they mean to give,
Is to deceive our selves, and not to live.
Hope lost, breeds grief, grief pain, and pain disease.
Our haps do turn as chances on the die.
Nor let him from his hope remove,
That under him, hath moved the stars above.
Hope and have, in time a man may gain any woman.
Hope well, fear not, mark this, be wise,
Droop not, for to despair, is to die twice.
Bad haps are help with hap and good belief.
O Hope, how cunning with our cares to gloze?
Griefs breathing point, the true man to desire,
The rest in sighs, the very thoughts repose,
As thou art mild, oh wert thou not a lier?
Fair speaking flattery subtle soothing guile:
Ah Hope, in thee our sorrows sweetly smile.
