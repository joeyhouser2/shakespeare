All art is learned by art, this art alone
It is a heavenly gift: no flesh nor bone
Can preise the honey we from Pind distil,
Except with holy fier his brest we fill.
From that spring flows, that men of special choose
Consumed in learning and perfit in prose:
For to make verse in vain doe is travel take,
When as a prentice fairer words will make.
Whilom in ages past none might profess
But princes and hie priests that sacred skill:
The sacred laws wherein they wont express,
And with deep oracles their verses fill,
Then was he held in sovereign dignity,
And made the nursling of nobility.
But now nor Prince nor Priest doth her maintain,
But suffer her prophaned for to be,
Of the base vulgar that with hands unclean
Dares to pollute her hidden mystery.
And treadeth underfoot her holy things,
Which was the care of Keysars and of Kings.
Those numbers wherewith heaven and earth are moved,
Shew, weakness speaks in prose, but power in verse.
—Man from man must holy parted be,
If with his age his verse do well agree.
Amongst our hands, he must his wits resing,
A holy traunce to highest heaven him bring.
For even as humane fury makes the man
Less then the man: so heavenly fury can
Make man pass man, and wander in holy mist
Upon the fiery heaven to walk at list.
Within that place the heavenly Poets sought
Their learning, sin to us here down it brought.
With verse that ought to Atropos no due,
Dame Natures trunchmen, heavens interpret true,
The vaunted verse a vacant head demands,
Ne wont with crabbed care the Muses dwell,
Unwisely weaves that takes two webs in hand.
O peerless Poesy, where is then thy place?
If not in princes palace thou doest sit,
And yet is princes palace the most fit.
Or breach of baser birth doth thee embrace,
Then make thee wings of thy aspiring wit,
And whence thou camest fly back to heaven apace.
All art is learned by art, but poesy
It is a gift divine, and cannot die.
Like as into the wax the seals imprent,
Is like a seal: right so the Poet gent,
Doth grave so vive in us his passions strange,
As makes the reader half in author change,
For Verses force is like that softly slides,
Through secret poris, and in our senses bides,
As make them have both good and ill imprented,
Which by the learned work is represented.
— Only he of Laurel is condign,
Who wisely can with profit pleasure minge,
The fairest walking on the sea coast been,
And surest swimming where the braes are green,
So wise is he who in his verse can have
Skill mixt with pleasure sports, with doctrine grave.
Who ever casts to compass weighty prise,
And thinks to throw out thundering words of threat:
Let power in lavish cups and thrifty bits of meat,
For Bacchus fruit is friend to Phoebus wise,
And when with wine the brain begins to sweat,
The numbers flow, as freely spring doth rise.
Riddled poesies and those significantly flow,
Differ in ears, as do in mouths the apricock and sloe.
What reason moved the golden Augustine
To name our Poetry vain errors wine?
Or Hierome deeply sighted in their evils,
To term it nothing but the food of devils.
Nought but the misemployment of our gifts,
Ordained for Art, but spent in shamlesse slufts.
Look as the sunbeam in a burning glass,
Doth kindle fier where ever it doth pass,
But freely spread upon th'engendring earth,
Eggs on the spring, and bills the cause of dearth,
So Poesy restrained in errors bounds,
With poisoned words and sinful sweetness wounds,
But clothing vertue and adorning it,
Wit shines in vertue, vertue shines in it.
