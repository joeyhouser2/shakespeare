In woods, in waves, in wars doth honour dwell,
And will be found with petill and with pain:
Ne can the man that moulds in idle cell
Unto her happy mansion attain,
Before her gate hie God did sweat ordain,
And wakeful watches ever to abide.
Honour is purchas'de by the deeds we do.
—Honour is not won
Until some honourable deed be done.
Danger bids seek the softest way one way.
But what saith honour? honour saith not so.
Never retire with shame; this honour saith:
The worst that can befall one, is but death.
In brave pursuit of honourable deed,
There is I know not what great difference
Betweene the vulgar and the common seed,
Which unto things of valerous pretence
Seems to be borne by native influence:
As feats of arms, and love to entertain,
But chiefly skill to ride, doth seem a science,
Proper to gentle blood; some others faine.
To manage steed. etc.
—Ever great imployment for the great,
Quickens the blood, and honour doth beget.
—Promotion is a puff,
These worldly honours are but shades of sweet:
Who seek too much before they get enough,
Before they meet the mean, with death they meet.
With death they meet the haven of all desire,
Where will must wa•ne, and pride cannot aspire.
Honour a thing without us, not our own.
What doth avail to have a princely place,
A name of honour, and an high degree:
To come by kindred of a noble race,
Except we princely worthy noble be,
The fruit declares the goodness of the tree.
Do brag no more of birth or linage than,
Sith vertue, grace, and manners make the man.
Search all thy books, and thou shalt find therein,
That honour is more hard to hold then win.
Defeated honour never more is to be got again.
—Vile is honour, and a little vain,
The which true worth and danger do not gain.
Vertue can bear what can on vertue fall.
Who cheapneth honour must not stand on price.
It most behoves the honourable race
Of mighty peers, true wisdom to sustain:
And with their noble countenance to grace
The learned foreheads without gifts or gain.
Or rather learned themselves behoves to be,
That is the garland of nobility.
—If that honour have one minutes stain,
An hundred years scant can it cleanse again.
A shame to fetch our long discent from kings,
And from great Jove derive our pedigree:
The brave atchiements of an hundred things,
Breathing vain boasts the world to terrify,
If we our selves do blot with infamy.
And stain the right and honour that is theirs,
Men cannot leave their virtues to their heirs.
Honour is grounded on the tickle yce.
The purest lawn most apt for every spot.
Honours shade, thrusts honours substance from his place.
Honour by due right is virtues hire.
Honours without imployment of estate,
Are like to sunbeam without heat or light:
A noble man and not a magistrate
Shines half eclipsed in his clearest bright.
Join heavenly gifts to earthly, light to light.
Let these great excellencies make a truce,
Fortune shall need no wheelwright for her use.
Great is the choise that grows in youthful mind,
When honour falls at variance with affection:
Nor could it yet be known or well definde,
Which passion keeps the other in subjection.
Both do allure, both doth the judgements blind,
Both do corrupt the heart with strong infection.
Yet lo sometimes these hurts procure our weal,
Even as one poison doth another heal.
The fiery sparkling precious Chrysolite
Spangled with gold, doth most transplendent shine:
The pearl graced by the ring, the ring by it,
The one, the others beauty doth refine:
And both together beauties do combine.
The jewel decks the golden hair that wears it,
Honour decks learning, that with honour rears it.
The inward touch that wounded honour bears,
Rests closely rankling, and can find no ease,
Till death of one side cure this great disease.
