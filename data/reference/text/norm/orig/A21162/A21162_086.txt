The higher that the Ceder tree, unto the heavens do growe,
The more in daunger is the top, when sturdie winds gan blowe,
Who Judges then in princely throne, to be devoide of hate,
Doeth not yet knowe, what heapes of ill, lies hid in such estate.
Such dangers great, such gripes of minde, such toile do they sustain
That often tymes, of God they wishe, to be unkyngde again.

For as the huge and mightie rockes, withstande the ragyng seas,
So kyngdomes in subiection be, whereas dame Fortune please:
Of brittle joye, of smilyng cheare, of Honie mixte with Gall,
Alotted is to every Prince, in freedome to be thrall.
What watches long, what steps unsure, what grefes and care of minde
What bitter broiles, what endlesse toiles, to kyngdomes be assingde.

The subiect then maie well compare, with prince for pleasant daies,
Whose silent nighe bryngs quiet rest, whose steps no storme bewraies:
How much be we, then bounde to God, who such provision makes,
To late our cares upon the Prince, thus doeth he for our sakes.
To hym therefore, let us lifte up our harts, and praie a maine,
That every Prince that he hath plast, maie long in quiet raigne.
