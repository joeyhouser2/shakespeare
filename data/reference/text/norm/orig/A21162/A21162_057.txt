IF that each flower, the Gods have framed, or shapt by sacred skill,
Where as I would (no wrong to wishe) and mine to weare at will.
Or els each tree, with lustie top, would lende me leave to Love,
With spriggs displaied to spread my sute, a wailyng harte to prove.
Upon my helme sone should you see, my hedde aduaunced hie,
Some slipp for solace there to set, and weare the same would I.
Yet would I not for greate delight, the Daises straunge desire,
The Lillie would not like my luste, nor Rose would I require.
The Marigolde might growe for me, Rosemarie well might reste,
The Fenell to, that is more fitt, for some unfrendly geste.
Nor Cowslopps would I crave at all, sometymes they seem to coye,
Some joly youth the Gelliflower, estemeth for his joye.
The Lavender sometymes alofte, alures the lookers eyes,
The Paunsie shall not have the praise, where I maie give the prise.
And thus no flower my fansie feedes, or liketh so my luste,
As that I maie subiecte my self, to toyes of tickle truste.
For flowers though they be faire and freshe, of sent excellyng sweete,
Yet growe they on the grounde belowe, we tread them with our feete.
And shall I then go stoupe to such? or els go seke to those,
Shall flowers enforce me once to faune, for feare of frends or foze.
Yet rather yelde I to the right, as reason hath assinde,
Mine aucthour saied there was no salve, in flowers for me to finde.
And yet perhaps some Tree there is, to shroude me from the shower,
That with her armes maie salve the soule, that yeldeth to her power.
Where I maie finde some pleasaunt shade, to salve me from the Sunne
Each thyng we see that reason hath, unto the Trees do runne.
Bothe men and beasts such foules as flyes, the treasures are the trees,
And for my parte when braunches fall, I wishe no other fees.
But when that stormes besett me rounde, such succour God me sende.
That I maie finde a frendlie Tree, that will me well defende.
No Tree there is which yelds no good, to some that doeth it seke:
And as they are of diverse kinds, their uses are unlike.
The Eve tree serves the Bowyers tourne, the Ashe the Coupers arte,
The puissaunt Oke doeth make the poste, the Pine some other parte.
The Elme doeth helpe to hide the birds, in wearie Winters night,
The Briers I gesse are nothyng worthe, they serve but for despight.
The Willowe wisht I farre from hence, good will deserve no wrong,
The Sallowe well maie serve their states, that syng so sad a song.
The Boxe and Beeche each for hym self, above the reste doeth boste,
The Eglantine for pleasure ofte, is pricked upon the poste,
The Hauthorne so is had in prise, the Baies do beare the bell,
And that these Baies did bryng no blisse, I like it not so well.
As erst I do that semely Tree, by which those Baies I founde,
And where withall unwittyngly, I tooke so greate a wounde.
As if the Tree by which I lent, doeth lende me no relief,
There is no helpe but doune I fall, so greate is growne my grief.
And therefore at the laste I crave, this favour for to finde,
When every Tree that here is tolde, beginns to growe unkinde.
The B. for beautie whom I boste, and shall above the reste,
That B. maie take me to her trust, for B. doeth please me beste.
It liks me well to walke the waie, where B. doeth keepe her bower,
And when it Raines to B. I runne to save me from the shower.
This braunche of B. which here I meane, to kepe and chiefly crave,
At becke unto this B. I bowe, to serve that beautie brave.
What shall I saie the tyme doeth passe, the tale to tedious is,
Though lothe to leave, yet leave I must, and saie no more but this.
I wishe this B. I might embrace, when as the same I see,
A league for life then I require, betwene this B. and me.
And though unworthy, yet good will, doeth worke the waie herein
And B. hath brought the same about, which beautie did begin.
