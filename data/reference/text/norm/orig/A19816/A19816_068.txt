Come tell me Titius eu'ry circumstaunce
How Cleopatra did receive my newes:
Tell eu'ry looke, each gesture, countenaunce,
That she did in my Letters reading use.
I shall my Lord so farre as I could note,
Or my conceite observe in any wise.
It was the time when as she having got
Leave to her Deerest dead to sacrifize;
And now was issuing out the Monument,
With Odors, Incense, Garlands in her hand,
When I approcht (as one from Caesar sent,)
And did her close thy message t'understand.
She turnes her backe, and with her takes me in,
Reades in thy lynes thy strange unlookt for tale:
And reades, and smyles, and stayes, and doth begin
Againe to reade, then blusht, and then was pale.
And having ended with a sigh, refoldes
Thy Letter up: and with a fixed eye,
(Which stedfast her imagination holds)
She mus'd a while, standing confusedly.
At length. Ah friend, (saith she,) tell thy good Lord,
How deere I hold his pittying of my case:
That out of his sweet nature can afford,
A miserable woman so much grace.
Tell him how much my heavy soule doth grieve
Mercilesse Caesar should so deale with me:
Pray him that he would all the counsell give,
That might divert him from such crueltie.
As for my love, say Anthony hath all,
Say that my hart is gone into the grave
With him, in whom it rests and ever shall:
I have it not my selfe, nor cannot have.
Yet tell him, he shall more commaund of me
Then any, whosoever lyving, can.
He that so friendly shewes himselfe to be
A right kind Roman, and a Gentleman.
Although his Nation (fatall unto me,)
Have had mine age a spoyle, my youth a pray,
Yet his affection must accepted be,
That favours one distrest in such decay.
Ah, he was worthy then to have been lou'd,
Of Cleopatra whiles her glory lasted;
Before she had declyning fortune prou'd,
Or scene her honor wrackt, her flower blasted.
Now there is nothing left her but disgrace,
Nothing but her affliction that can move:
Tell Dolabella, one that's in her case,
(Poore soule,) needes rather pitty now then love.
But shortly shall thy Lord heare more of me.
And ending so her speech, no longer stayd,
But hasted to the Tombe of Anthony.
And this was all she did, and all she said.
Ah sweet distressed Lady. What hard hart
Could chuse but pitty thee, and love thee too?
Thy worthines, the state wherein thou art
Requireth both, and both I vow to do.
Although ambition lets not Caesar see
The wrong he doth thy Maiestie and sweetnes,
Which makes him now exact so much of thee,
To add unto his pride, to grace his greatnes.
He knowes thou canst no hurt procure us now,
Sith all thy strength is ceaz'd into our hands:
Nor feares he that, but rather labours how
He might shew Rome so great a Queene in bands.
That our great Ladies (enuying thee so much
That stain'd then all, and hell'd them in such wonder,)
Might joy to see thee, and thy fortune such,
Thereby extolling him that brought thee under.
But I will seeke to stay it what I may;
I am but one, yet one that Caesar loves,
And ô if now I could do more then pray,
Then should'st thou know how far affection moves.
But what my powre and prayer may prevaile,
Ile joyne them both, to hinder thy disgrace:
And even this present day I will not fayle
To do my best with Caesar in this case.
And Sir, even now her selfe hath Letters sent,
I met her messenger as I came hither,
With a dispatch as he to Caesar went,
But knowes not what imports her sending thither.
Yet this he told, how Cleopatra late
Was come from sacrifice. How ritchly clad
Was seru'd to dinner in most sumptuous state,
With all the bravest ornaments she had.
How having dyn'd, she writes, and sends away
Him straight to Caesar, and commaunded than
All should depart the Tombe, and none to stay
But her two maides, and one poore Countryman.
Why then I know, she sends t'have audience now,
And meanes t'experience what her state can do:
To see if Maiestie will make him bow
To what affliction could not move him to.
And ô, if now she could but bring a view
Of that fresh beauty she in youth possest,
(The argument where-with she over-threw
The wit of Julius Caesar, and the rest,)
Then happily Augustus might relent,
Whilst powrefull Love, (far stronger then ambition)
Might worke in him, a mind to be content
To graunt her asking, in the best condition.
But beeing as she is, yet doth she merite
To be respected, for what she hath been:
The wonder of her kinde, of rarest spirit,
A glorious Lady, and a mighty Queene.
And now, but by a little weakenes falling
To do that which perhaps sh'was forst to do:
Alas, an error past, is past recalling,
Take away weakenes, and take wemen too.
But now I go to be thy Aduocate,
Sweet Cleopatra, now Ile use mine arte.
Thy presence will me greatly animate,
Thy face will teach my tongue, thy love my hart.
