IF that each flower, the Gods have framed, or shapt by sacred skill,
Where as I would (no wrong to wish) and mine to weare at will
Or els ech tree, with lustie top, would lend me leave to love.
With sprigs displaied to spred my sute, a wayling hart to prove.
Upon my helme some should you see, my head aduaunced hye.
Some slip for solace there to set, and weare the same would I:
Yet would I not for great delight, the Daises straunge desire,
The Lillie would not like my lust, nor Rose would I require.
The Marigolde might growe for me, Rosemarie well might rest,
The Fenell to, that is more fit: for some unfriendly gest:
Nor Cowslops would I crave at all, sometime they seeme too coye,
Some joly youth the Gelliflower, esteemeth for his joye.
The Lavender sometimes aloft, allures the lookers eyes,
The Paunsie shall not have the prayse, where I may give the prise.
And thus no flower my fansie feedes, or liketh so my lust,
As that I may subiect my selfe, to toyes of tickle trust.
For flowers though they be faire and fresh, of sent excelling sweete,
Yet grow they on the ground below, we tread them with our feete:
And shall I then go stoupe to such? or els go seeke to chose,
Shall flowers enforce me once to faune, for feare of friendes or foes.
Yet rather yeeld I to the right, as reason hath assignd,
Mine author said there was no salve, in flowers for me to finde:
And yet perhaps some Tree there is, to shrowd me from the shower,
That with her armes may salve the soule, that yeeldeth to her power.
Where I may finde some pleasaunt shade, to salve me from the sunne,
Each thing we see that reason hath, unto the Trees do runne:
Both men and beastes such foules as flies, the treasures are the Trees,
And for my part when braunches fall, I wish no other fees.
But when that stormes beset me round, such succour God me send,
That I may finde a friendly Tree, that will me well defend:
No Tree there is which yeeldes no good, to some that doth it seeke,
And as they are of divers kindes, their uses are unlike.
The Ewe Tree serve the Bowyers tourne, the Ashe the Coupers arte.
The puissant Oke doth make the poste, the Pine some other parte:
The Elme doth helpe to hide the birdes, in wearie Winters night,
The Briers I gesse are nothing worthe, they serve but for despight.
The willow wisht I farre from hence, good will deserve no wrong,
The Sallow well may serve their states, that sing so sad a song:
The Boxe and Beeche each for himselfe, above the rest doth boste,
The Eglantine for pleasure oft, is pricked upon the poste.
The Hauthorne is so sad in price, the Baies do beare the bell,
And that these Baies did bring no blisse, I like it not so well:
As erst I do that seemely Tree, by which those bayes I found,
And wherewithall unwittingly, I tooke so great a wound,
As if the tree by which I leane, doth lend me no reliefe,
There is no helpe but downe I fall, so great is growne my griefe:
And therefore at the last I crave, this favour for to finde,
when every tree that here is tolde, begins to grow unkinde.
The B. for beautie whome I boste, and shall above the rest,
That B. may take me to her trust, for B. doth please me best:
It likes me well to walke the way, where B. doth keepe her bower,
And when it raynes to B. I run, to save me from the shower:
This braunch of B. which here I meane, to keepe and chiefly crave,
At becke unto this B. I bow, to serve that beautie brave:
What shall I say the time doth passe, the tale to tedious is,
though soth to leave, yet leave I must, and say no more but this,
I wish this B. I might embrace, when as the same I see,
A league for life then I require, betwene this B. and me?
And though unworthy, yet good will, doth worke the way herein,
And B. hath brought the same above, which beautie did begin.
