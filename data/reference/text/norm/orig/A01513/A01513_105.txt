Resigne king Pryams sonnes, that princes were in Tr•y.
Resigne to me your happie dayes, and boast no more of joy:
Sir Paris first stand forth, make aunswere for thy pheare,
And if thou canst defend her cause, whome Troy did bye so deare:
What? blush not man, be bold, although thou beare some blame,
Tell truth at last, and so be sure to save thy selfe from shame.
Then gentle Shepheard sat: what madnesse did thee move
To choose of all the flowres in Greece, foule Helene for thy love?
Needes must I coumpt her foule, whose first frutes wer forlorne
Although she solde her second chaffe, above the price of corne.
Alas, she made of thee, a noddye for the nonce,
For Menelaus lost her twice, though thou her foundst but once.
But yet if in thine eye, she seemd a peerelesse peece,
Aske Theleu• the mighty Duke, what towns she knew in Greece?
Aske him what made her leave her wofull aged sire,
And steale to Athens gyglot like: what? what but foule desire?
Alas pore Paris thou didst nothing else but gleane
The partched eares which he cast by, when he had reaped cleane:
He sliude the gentle Slippe, which could both twist and twind,
And growing left the broken braunch, for then that came behind.
Yet hast thou filld the worlde with brute, the more thy blame,
And saist, that Hellens bewty past each other stately dame.
For proofe thou canst alledge the tast of ten yeares warre,
And how her blasing beames first brought both Greece and Tr•y to jarre:
No no, thou art deceiude, the drugs of foule despite
Did worke in Menelaus will, not losse of such delighte,
Not love but lothsome hate, not dolour but disdayne,
Did make him seeke a sharpe revenge, til both is foes wer slaine.
Thy brother Troylus eke, that gemme of gentle deedes,
To thinke how he abused was, alas my heart it bleedes:
He bet about the bush, whiles other caught the birds,
Whome craftie Cresside mockt to much, yet fed him still with words.
And God he knoweth not I, who pluckt her first sprong rose,
Since Lollius and Chauser both, make doubt uppon that glose.
But this I know too well, and he to farre it felt,
How Diomede undid his knots, and caught both brooch and belt,
And how she chose to change, and how she changed still,
And how she died leaper like, against her lovers will.
Content you then good knights, your triumphe to resigne,
Confesse your starres both dimme and darke, wheras my sunne doth shine:
For this I dare avow, without vaunt be it told,
My derling is more faire than she, for whome proud Troy was solde.
More constant to conteine, than Cresside to be coy,
No Calcas can contrive the craft, to traine her out of Troy,
No Diomede can draw her settled hart to change,
No madding moode can move her mind, nor make her thoughts to range,
For her alone it is, that Cupide blindfold goes,
And dare not looke for feare least he his libertie should loose:
At her dame Venus chafes, and pines in jelowsie,
Least bloudy Mars should her espie, and change his fantasie.
Of her the Queene of Heaven doth stand in dreadfull doubte,
Least •ove should melt in drops of gold, if once he find her out.
Oh that my tong had skill, to tell her praise aright,
Or that my pen her due deserts, in worthy verse could write:
Or that my mind could muse, or happie hart conceive,
Some wor•s that might resound her worth, by high Mineruas leave.
Oh how the blooming joyes, do blossome in my brest,
To thinke within my secret thought, howe farre she steynes the rest.
Me thinks I heare her speake, me thinks I see her still,
Me thinks I feele her feelingly, me thinks I know her will.
Me thinks I see the states, which sue to her for grace,
Me thinks I see one looke of hirs repulse them all apace.
Me thinks that houre is yet, and evermore shall be,
Whereine my happie happe was first, her heavenly face to see:
Wherein I spide the writte, which woond betweene her eyne,
And said behold, be bold, for I, am borne to be but thine.
Me thinks I feele the joyes which never yet was felt,
Whome flame before yet never toucht, me thinks I feele them melt.
One word and there an end: me thinks she is the sunne,
Which only shineth now a dayes, she dead, the world wer done.
The rest are twinckling starres, or Moones which borrow light
To comfort other carefull soules, which wander in the night.
And night God knowes it is, where other ladies be,
For sure my dame adornes the day, there is no sunne but she.
Then lovers by your leave, and thinke it nothing straunge
Although I seeme with calme content, in Seas of joyes to range:
For why, my sailes have sound both wind and waves at will,
And depths of all delights in her, with whome I travell still charge.
And ancors being wayed, I leave you all at large,
To steare this seemely Shippe my selfe, such is my mistresse
