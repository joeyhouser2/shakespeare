J Thought good fayth, and durst have gagde my hand
For you (Friend T.) the beautie should now hight
Have rasde your hart, nor Cupid with his brand
Have brought thy learned breast to such a plight.
I thought Mineruas gift had been of powre
By holesome reade to roote this fansie out:
But now I see that Venus in an howre
Can bend the best, and dawnt the wise and stoute.
Why shouldst thou seeke to make the Tiger tame?
To win a Woulfe so cruell by his kinde?
To suffer Aesops Snake thou art to blame
That stoong the man where he reliefe did finde.
Is naught in her but Womans name alone,
No Woman sure she is, but Monster fell,
That scornes her friend, and makes him die with mone.
Who makes an Idoll of a Divell of Hell.
She was cut out of some Sea beaten rock,
Or taken from the cruell Lyons Tet,
That feedes her Friend for friendship with a mock
And smiles to see him macht in Follies Net.
If thou were wise (as thou art full of love)
Thou wouldst account her beautie but a Glasse,
And from thy hart such fansies fond remove
I loth to see the Lyon wex an Asse.
If so she were thy faithfull Friend in deede,
And sought a salve to cure thy cruell sore,
(As now she seekes to make thy hart to bleede)
Good fayth thou couldst account of her no more.
But waying now her great abuse to thee
A Friend to her, but to thy selfe a Foe:
Why shouldst thou love, or so enamoured be?
Leave off be time, let all such dotage go.
Should I imbrace the man that hates my life?
Should I account of him that settes me light?
Should I yeeld up my throate to murthring Knife?
Or seeke for to reclaime a Haggard Kite?
Hast thou not read how wise Ulysses did
Enstuffe his eares with Waxe, and close them up,
Of Cyrces filthie love himselfe to rid,
That turnd his Mates to Swine by Witches cup?
And how he did the lyke upon the Seas
The pleasant noysome Syrens songs tendure,
That otherwise had wrought him great unease
If once they mought his mates and him allure?
Put thou the Greekes devise againe in ure,
Stop up thine cares this Syren to beguile,
Seale up those wanton eies of thine, be sure
To lend no eare unto her flattring stile.
For all her talke but to deceit doth tende,
A canckred hart is wrapt in friendly lookes:
She all her wittes to thy decay doth bende,
Thou art the Fish, she beares the byting hookes.
No savage beast doth force a man a whit
That loves him not: we see the dogged Curre
Fawnes not one him that with the whip doth smite
The Horse hates him that pricks him with the spurre.
And wilt thou love, or place within thy brest
The cruell Dame that weaves thy web of woe?
Wilt thou still fawne upon so false a guest:
In stead of Dove wilt thou retaine a Crowe?
Beware in time, ere Beautie pierce to farre,
Let fansies go, love where is love againe:
For doubtlesse now to much to blame you arre.
To sowe good will and reape but fowle disdaine.
I counsaile thus that may thee best aduise,
For that my selfe did serve a cruell Dame:
The blinde recurde can judge of bleared eies,
The Criple healde knowes how to heale the lame.
Shake thou betimes the yoke from off thy neck,
For feare the print thereof remaine behind:
A happie man is he that feares no check,
But lives at freedome with contented minde.
