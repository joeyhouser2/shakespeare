You troubled mindes with tormentes toste,
that sighes and sobs consumes:
(Who breathes and puffes from burning breast,
both smothring smoke and fumes.)
Come reade this booke that freelye bringes, a boxe of balme full swete,
An oyle to noynt the brused partes, of everye heavye spriete.
A souplinge salve for everye sore, a medcine for the sicke,
A seede that eates up cankred fleshe, and searcheth neare the quicke.
Each griefe the growes by error blinde (that makes mans judgement jarre)
May here a precious plaster finde, eare corsye creepe to farre.
The blinde that mournes for want of sight, coulde he but heare this red,
Would take his blindnes in good part, and beare a quiet hed.
The lame whose lacke of legges is death, unto a loftye mynde,
Will kisse his crotche and creepe on knees, Cardanus woorkes to fynde.
The begger bare bedeckt in brats, and patched rotten rags,
In budget if he bare this booke, would scorne the roysters brags.
The shepehearde that in skortchinge sunne, sits skowling on the skyes,
Would leave the wolfe his flocke of sheepe, to see this booke with eyes.
The surlye snodge that sweepes up golde, and makes his God thereon,
Would sure confesse this pearle shold shyne, when glistring gold were gon.
The wyldest man or monster strange, whose natures naughtye are,
Would stand a ma•de as bucke at baye, uppon this booke to stare,
This is no fable finelye fylde, as cutlare workes the blade,
This is a substance of it selfe, this is no sillye shade.
This speakes out of the brasen heade, full many a golden word,
This strykes the stordye stomackes dead, and yet it drawes no sworde.
This threatens thonderboltes for fooles, yet weather fayre it showes,
So such as can beare of a storme, and calmye weather knowes.
This teacheth men to tune their strings, who would sweete musicke make
This showes who faynes, or sweetely sings: and where the tune we take.
The poore that playnes on pinching plagues, by this doth stand content,
And yeldinge thankes for foode and cloth, takes well the God hath sent.
The rich whose raging reach would reape, the sweete of every soyle,
Shall learne to singe a mixrye meane, and leave the poore the spoyle.
The hye or hautye hart shall here, a livelye lesson learne,
How wysedome holdes himselfe upright, and halting heades deserne.
The lowe that lours at lothsome locke, and lingers out his tyme,
Shall see how safe the simple si•s, and how they fall that clyme.
The strong that strives to winne the goale, by strength and stoutnes vaine,
Shall shunne the shouldring croked play, and walke the path full plaine.
The weake whose wits with woes are worne (which breedes in brest debare)
Shall laughe the giants strength to scorne, and prayse the feeble state,
The sicke that seekes a syrope sweete, for soure disease wythin,
Shall helpe the heapes of harmes in hart, eare blister rise on skin.
The proude the poultes and pickes his plumes, and prunes his fethers gay,
Shall meekenes showe and forthwyth fling, his painted sheath away.
The prisner that in fetters lyes, shall thincke his fredome more,
In closed walles than all his scoope, that he hath had before,
The banisht wight that beates his braynes, with many busy broyles,
Shall see what gaine exile doth bringe, by sight of sondrye soyles.
The seruaunt that in seruage lyves, shall fee he hath more ease,
Than hath his maister who of force, must many people please.
The fearefull man that hateth death, shall see that death is best,
And death is most to be desyrde, where life can breede no rest.
The dronken dolt that doth delite, in sosse, in swashe, and swill,
Shall see some snib or soure rebuke, to breake him of his will.
The foole that all sound counsell hates, perhaps in reading this,
Maye waxe more wyse and fondnes leave, and so amende the mis.
The flatterer here may finde his faults, and fall to better frame,
The currishe earle may civill be, in noting of the same.
The cowarde shall win courage great, as he this booke shall vewe,
And he that is not shaped right, may here be made a newe.
The plowman that with sweat of browes, doth dearely win his bred.
Shall see what daunger dwell they in, that are with daintyes fed.
There is no state that beareth lyfe, of hye or lowe degree,
But for the sickenes of his minde, a medsine here may see.
This booke bewrayes what wretched wracke, belongs to life of man,
What burthens bore he on his backe, since first this world began.
This is a glasse to gaze upon, where man himselfe may finde,
A shyning sunne that plainlye shewes, A man is but his minde,
And who that reads and marks a right, the reasons couched here,
Shall win such treasures by the same, as he shall hold ful dere.
Passe on plaine booke of pearelesse price, and preace in worthye place,
Dread no disdaine of froward heads, nor feare the frowning face.
A worthye worke doth justly crave, a worthye patrone still,
Whose noble bucklar shall defende, this worthye worke from in.
And he that made thee Englishe speake, his tongue and penne be blest,
With happye hope of vertues hye, from heaven, here possest.
