Gyve wyt and woful voyce, O Syre, let me my woes complayne.
For this the moone hath hyd her face, and thrise come backe agayn.
Syth first I set me downe, in slouth and sobbinge cheare
No muse to comfort care.
What man can make a spring of teares, to feede my gulfe of griefe
Or who hath store of teares so great: and far from all reliefe.
Pitye hath bereft my sight, and hart hath cleft in twayne,
Which suffereth not my woes to sound, my tong cannot complayn
Such is my griefe.
Loe now my study stayde is, for cruell death have slaine.
My brother deare: shall I pore wretch in wretched life remayne?
The only hope of all our house, O death thou hast bereft me
Mine earthly joy this brother was, none other joy is left me.
My countrye wrought my woe, my frendes dyd hate me all,
If death had tane my giltles soule, no griefe had made me thrall.
Lo yet among you men I live, and styll enioy this lyght
But long I may not so.
Your deadly darts (O foes) for pittye cast in me:
With cruell sworde before the rest, let me destroyed be.
Els thou Almightye God, on me such mercye have,
As that my wretched head may rest, within mine earthly grave.
But him a carefull cloud, did compasse rounde about,
And on his head with heavy hand, the dust he poured out.
And in my greenest yeares, when youth hath hyest power,
Shall this my spirite depart awaye, and death my corps devoure?
The Gods I cannot guide, their will we must obay,
Where destny dryves I yeld my selfe: with willing mind alway.
Is not our sleape (O foole) of death, an Image playne?
For fatall course shall bring a rest, that ever will remayne.
Nowe eate, nowe drinke, nowe make good sport:
For sith thy felfe on earthe, a mortall man do see
Take here thy fill of earthly joyes, no joyes hereafter be.
In fortunes high disgrace: each man may death disdayne.
But he most valiaunt is, that can, in wretched state remayne.
And as twise twentye yeares bypast, so nowe my force I finde
Mine aged yeares are worne away, I feele my youthful minde.
For eyther soone or late, in order as men saye,
The wretched flocke of wordly folke, to death do take their way.

Do neyther seke nor shonne: the ende of thine own lyfe.
Thou dastard knight, Damatrion, thy mother here have slayne.
That doest both her, and Spartayn bloud: with cowerdice distayne
Although unweldyage, when lyfe doth weare away,
None other ill did bring withall, but that, (as men do say)
By livinge long ful oft we see: wich we would not behold.
It seemeth wel that cowerds wepe, when they be brought to grave
But thou my son a Spartayn true, no weping teares shalt have.
