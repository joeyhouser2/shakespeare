IN Babilon a stately seate, of high and mighty Kinges,
Whose famous voice of ancient rule, through all the world yet ringes:
Two great estates did whilom dwell: and places joyned so,
As but one wall each princely place, devided other fro:
These Nobles two, two children had, for whom Dame Nature sought,
The deepest of her secret skill, or she their byrth had wrought:
For as their yeares in one agreed, and beauty equall shone,
In bounty and lyke vertues all, so were they there all one.
And as it pleased Nature then, the one a sonne to frame,
So did the glad olde Father like him Pyramus to name:
Th'other a maide, the mother would that she then Thisbie hight,
With no smal blisse of parents all, who came to joy the sight:
I overslip what sodaine frights, how often feare there was,
And what the care each creature had, ere they did overpas:
What paynes ensue, and what the stormes in pearced harts that dwel,
And therfore know, what babe and mother whose chast, and subtil brand
No earthly hart, ne when they lust, no God hath yet withstand,
Ere seven yeres these infants harts, they have with love opprest:
Though litle know their tender age, what causeth their unrest,
Yet they poore fooles untaught to love, or how to lesse their payne:
With well contented mindes receive, and prime of love sustayne.
No pastime can they elswhere finde, but twayn themselves alone
For other playfeares sport, God wot, with them is reckend none:
Joy were to here their prety wordes, and sweet mamtam to see,
And how all day they passe the time, till darknes dimmes the skye:
But then the heavy cheare they make, when forst is their farwell
Declares such greefe as none would thinke, in so yong brests could dwell:
Ye looke how long, that any let, doth kepe them two a sunder,
Their mourning harts no joy may glad, that hevens the passeth under
And when agayn, they efte repayre, and joyfull meeting make,
Yet know they not the cause therof, ne why their sorowes slake.
With sight they feede their fancies then, and more it still de••re,
Ye more they have, nor want they finde of sight they so require:
And thus in tender impe spronge up, this love upstarteth still,
For more their yeres, much more the flame, that doth their fancies fill.
And where before their infants age, gave no suspect at all.
Now needefull is, with weary eye, to watchfull minde they call:
Their whole estate, and it to guide, in such wise orderly,
As of their secret sweete desires, ill tongues no light espy.
And so they did, but hard God wot, are flames of fire to hide
Much more to cause a lovers hart, within it bounds to finde:
For neither colde, their mindes consent so quench of love the rage
Nor they at yeres, the least twise seven, their passions so aswage
But that to Thisbes Mothers eares, some spark therof were blowen,
Let Mothers judg her pacience now, til she the whole have knowen.
And so by wily wayes she wrought, to her no litle care,
That forth she found, their whole devise, and how they were in snare:
Great is her greefe, though smal the cause, if other cause ne were,
For why a meeter match then they, might hap no other where:
But now tween Fathers, though the cause, mine Auctor nothing els,
Such inward rancor risen is, and so it daily swels.
As hope of freendship to be had, is none (alas) the while,
Ne any loveday to be made, their mallice to begyle:
Wherfore straight charge, straight given is with fathers frowning chere,
That message worde, ne token els, what ever that it were:
Should fron their foe to Thisbee passe, and Pyramus freends likewise,
No lesse expresse commaundement, do for their sonne devise.
And yet not thus content alas, each Father doth ordayne.
A secret watch and bounde a point, wherin they shall remayne:
Sight is forbid, restrained are wordes, for scalde is all devise,
That should their poore afflicted mindes, reioyce in any wise:
Though pyning love, gave cause before of many carefull yll,
Yet dayly sithe amended all, at least well pleased them still:
But now what depth of deepe distresse, may they indrowned be,
That now in dayes twise twenty tolde, each other once shall see.
Curst is their face, so cry they ofte, and happy death they call,
Come death come wished death at once, and rid us life and all.
And where before (Dame Kinde) her selfe, did wonder to beholde
Her highe bequests within their shape, Dame Beauty did unfold:
Now doth she maruel much and say, how faded is that red?
And how is spent that white so pure, it wont to overspred.
For now late lusty Piramus, more fresh then flower in May,
As one forlorne with constant minde, doth seeke his ending day:
Since Thisbe mine is lost sayth he, I have no more to lose,
Wherfore make speed, thou happy hand, these eyes of mine shall close.
Abasid is his princely port, cast of his regall weede,
Forsaken are assemblies all, and lothed the foming steed:
No joy may pearce his pensive mynde, unlesse a wofull brest
May joyed be, with swarmes of care, in haples hart that rest:
And thus poore Piramus distrest, of humaine succor all,
Devoyd to Venus Temple goes, and prostrate downe doth fal:
And there of her, with hart I korue, and sore tormented mindes,
Thus askes he ayd, and of his woes, the Fardell thus unbindes.

O Great Goddesse, of whose immortal fire,
Uertue in Erbe, might never quench the flame:
Ne mortall sence, yet to such skill aspire,
As for loves hurt a medecine once to name:
With what deare price, my carefull pyned ghost,
Hath tried this true, and over true alas:
My greefeful eyes, that sight hath almost lost,
And brest through darted, with thy golden Mace.
Full well declare, though all that me beholde,
Are judges, and wonders of my deadly wo:
But thou alone, mayst helpe therfore unfolde,
Els helples (Lady) streight will knap in two
The feeble thread, yet stayes my lingering life.
Wherfore, if love, thy sacred Goddes brest?
Did ever presse, or if most dreadly griefe,
And causeles not thy inward soule opprest:
When crooked Vulcane, to your common shame,
Bewrayed of stolen joyes, thy sweet delight:
If then I say the feare of further blame,
Caus'd you refrayne your Lovers wished sight:
And forst restraynt did equall then impart,
And cause you taste, what payne in love may be:
When absence drives, assured hartes to part.
Thy pitty then (O Queene) now not denye
To me poore wretch, who feeles no lesse a payne:
If humayne brests, so much as heavenly may:
Have ruthe on him, who doth to thee complayne,
And only helpe of thee, doth lowly pray:
Graunt Goddesse mine, thou mayst it undertake,
At least wise (Lady) ere this life decay:
Graunt I beseeche so happy me to make,
That yet by worde, I may to her bewray
My wonderous woes: and then if ye so please,
Looke when you lust, let death my body ease.
Thus praying fast, ful fraught with cares, I leave this wofull man,
And turne I will to greater greefe, then minde immagin can:
But who now shall them writ since wit, denayeth the some to thinke,
Confusedly in Thisbies brest, that flow above the brinke?
Not, I for though of mine own store, I want no woes to write,
Yet lacke I termes and cunning both, them aptly to recite.
For Cunnings clyffe I never clombe, nor dranke of Science spring
Ne slept upon the happy hill, fron whence Dame Rhetorique rings.
And therfore all, I do omit, and wholy them resigne,
To judgment of such wofull Dames, as in like case hath bin.
This will I tel how Thisbie thus, opprest with dollors all,
Doth finde none ease but day and night, her Pyramus to call:
For lost is slepe and banisht is, all gladsome lightes delight,
In short of case and every helpe, each meane she hath in spight:
In langor long, this life she led, till hap as fortune pleased,
To further fates that fast ensue, with her own thought her eased:
For this she thinkes, what distance may, or mansions be between
Or where now stands so cruell wall, to part them as is seene
O feeble wit forduld with woe, awake thy wandering thought,
Seeke out, thou shalt assured finde, shall bring thy cares to nought.
With this some hope, nay, as it were a new revived minde,
Did promis straight her pensive hart, immediate helpe to finde:
And forth she steres, with swifted pace, ech place she seeks throughout
No stay may let her hasty foote, till all be vewed about.
Wherby at length from all the rest, a wall aloofe that lyes,
And corner wise did buyldings part, with joyful eye she spyes:
And scarcely then her pearcing looke, one blinke therof had got,
But that firme hope of good successe, within her fancy shot:
Then fast her eye she roules about, and fast she seekes to see,
If any meane may there be found, her comfort for to be:
And as her carefull looke she cast, and every part aright
Had vewed wel, a litle rifte appeared to her sight,
Which (as it seemed) through the wall, the course the issue had:
Wherwith she said (O happy wall) mayst thou so blist be made,
That yet sometimes within thy bandes, my dere hart Pyramus:
Thou doost possesse if hap so worke, I will assay thee thus.
And from about the heavenly shape, her midle did present
She did unlose heer girdle riche, and pendent therof hent.
And with her fingers long and small, on tipto so she wrought,
That through the wall to open sight, she hath the pendant brought
That doone she stayes, and to the wall she closely layes her eare,
To understand if any wight, on th' other side yet were:
And whiles to harken thus she stands, a wonderous thing behold
Poore Pyramus in Venus Church, that all his minde had tolde.
Performed his vowes and prayers eke, now ended all and dun,
Doth to his Chamber fast returne, with hart right wo begun:
Even to the same where Thisbie stayd, to see if fortune please,
To smooth her browes and her distresse, with any helpe to ease:
He as his woonted usage was, the Chamber once within,
Lockes fast the doore with fresh complaynts, new sorrow to begin.
But even lo as his backe he turned unto the closed dore,
Aglimpse of light the pendant gave, his visage just before:
Let in his face, with speedy pace, and as he nearer drew,
With wel contented minde forthwith, his Thisbies signe he knew
And when his trembling hand for joy, the same receyved had,
And he ten hundreth times it kist, then thus to it he said.
Though many tokens joyful newes have set,
And blisse redust, to carefull pyned ghost:
Yet mayst thou sweare, that never lyved he yet,
Who halfe such ease, received in pleasure most:
As thou sweete pendant, now in wofull brest
Impersid hast, O happy Pyramus,
Nay beeing a Lady, in whom such r•the can rest:
Most blisfull Lady, most mighty Venus,
And mighty Thisbie (yea) Venus not displeased,
My Goddesse cheefe, my love, my life and all:
For who but Thisbie would, nay could have eased,
A hart remedyles, abandon thrall:
Wherfore since thus ye please, to show your might,
Make me whole happy, with gladnesse of your sight.
Whiles Pyramus all clad in joy, thus talkes within the wall,
No lesse content, doth Thisbie stand without and heareth all:
And with those gladsom lightes, where love doth sightly joy to play,
And vanquish harts her love she vewes in minde somwhat to say
But maydenly feare plucks backe the word, dread stops her trimbling tongue,
A rossy hew inflames her face, with staine of red among.
Yet lo at length her minde thee stayes, her sences do awake,
And with a sweet soft sounding voyce, this answer doth she make.
Love Pyramus, more deare to me then lyfe,
Even as I first this way, for speech have found:
Of present death, so let the dreadfull knyfe,
At this instant for ever me consound:
If joyfull thought my passing pensive harte,
Did ever pearse, since parents cruell dome.
Pronounst the sentence, of our common smart,
No deare hart mine, for how alasse may blome:
The fading tree, whose sap devided is,
Ye, further sweet, I dare with you presume:
Your passed woes, but pastimes ware I wis,
In their respect, that did me whole consume.
But now sharpe sighes, so stop my willing speeche,
Such streames of teares, do dim my troubled sight:
And inward feare, of parents wrath is such,
Least longer talke, should give them any light
Of our repayre, that further to recyte,
My heaped yls I never dare ne may,
Yet oftenly, we wisely heare may meete:
At chosen times which shall us not bewray,
And this for short, thy Thisbie shalt thou see:
With morning light, here present eft to be,
To this full fayne would Pyramus, replyed have agayne,
But part as neede, inforst they must, and as they did ordayne:
Ere mornings dawne they do arise and straight repayre they then
Unto the fore appoynted place, Pyrame thus began.
Mine entyer soule, what prison dollours?
What hard distresse, and rare devysed woes?
Of me thine own, thy captive Pyramus,
Have so sought, this life from boddy to unlose:
Hard were to tell the tenth, that have it strained,
With thought hereof, great wonders me amaze:
How my poore lyfe, the halfe may have sustayned,
O Thisbie mine own, whom it only stayes.
And at whose will the fates do lend me breath,
Yet may I not the fatall stroke eschew:
Ne scape the dinte of fast pursuing death,
Onles your bounty, present mercy shew:
And this I trust, there may no jelous thought,
Have any place within my Thisbies brest:
To cause her deeme, I am or may be caught.
With love but hers wheron my life doth rest,
No be assured, for yours I only taste:
Yours was the first, and shall be first and last,
Why my most sweet (quoth Thisbie) then agayne:
I doubt not I, but know ye are all true,
Or how may cause of your undoubted payne:
With her be hyd, who hourely as it grew,
None other felt, but even what ye have had:
Yet thinke not sweet, I taste your greefes alone,
Or make esteeme, as ye of me have made,
But ten times more, if that more wo begone,
Might ever be a wretched maydens brest,
Where never yet, one jot of joy might rest.
Well then my joy, (quoth Pyrame) since ye please,
With so greater love, to guerdon my good will:
Safe am I now, but great were mine ease,
If more at full, I might my fancy fill:
With nearer sight, of your most pleasant face,
Or if I might, your dayntie fingers straine:
Or as I woont, your body once embrace,
What say I ease? nay heaven then were my gayne.
Howbeit in vayne, in vayne (ay me) I waste,
Both worde and winde, woes me (alas) therfore:
For never shall my hart, O Thisbie taste,
So great an hap, nor never shall we more:
In folded armes, as woont were to bewray,
Each others state, ne never get the grace:
Of any joy, unlesse we do assay,
To finde some meane for other meeting place.
Beholde (alas) this wicked cruell wall,
Whose cursed scyte, denayeth us perfect sight:
Much more the hap, of other ease at all.
What if I should by force, as well one might:
And yet deserves, it batter flat to ground,
And open so, an issue large to make:
Yet feare I sore, this sooner will redownde,
To our reproche, if it I undertake:
As glad I would, then us to helpe or ayde,
Sweet hart (quoth she) wherwith she stopt his tale:
This standes full yll: to purpose to be made,
And time it askes, too long for to prevayle:
Without suspect, to flat or batter even,
Naythlesse, ye this, or what ye can devise:
For our repayre, by thought that may be driven,
Say but the meane, I will none otherwise.
Ye Thisbie mine, in sooth, and say you so▪
(Quoth Pyramus) well then I do you know:
Where King Minus, lyes buried long ago,
Whose auncient Tombe above, doth overgrow
A Mulbery, with braunches making shade,
Of pleasant show, the place right large about:
There if ye please, when slepe hath overlade,
And with his might, the Cittie seas'de throughout:
At the same Well, whose silver streames then runne.
And softe as silke, conserve the tender greene:
With hue so fresh, as springtied spent and dunne,
No winters weede, hath power to be seene:
Without suspect, or feare of foule report,
There goddesse mine, we salfely may resort.
TO this she said, what she best thought, and oft and oft agayne,
Was talke renued, but yet at last, for ease of every payne:
And death to eschue by other meane, who will them not forsake,
At Minus Tombe, even the same night, they do their meeting make
And so depart, but fore God wot, that day doth them offend,
And though but short his long abode, the feare will never end.
And sooner doth not cloake of night, alofte his shadow cast,
But Thisbie mindefull of her love, and promis lately past
Of fresh new love, far fiercer flames, that erst her hart opprest,
She feelth the force, and this (alas) devorced stil from rest:
She passeth forth in carefull watch, till time have shapen so,
That s•epe with sweet, soft stealing steps his customd usage do
And when she seeth both house and all drownd therin fast and deepe,
With fearful pace and trimbling hand, she forwards gins to creepe:
She gaines the doore, out goeth she then, and neyther far ne neare,
Appeareth wyght save Phebe fayre, with gladsom seeming cheare
Sole Thisbie joyfull of this guyde, doth ay I trust it be,
Good lucke thy presence doth import, and bring at last to me:
More hardyer then before she did, provoke her foote to hast,
No obiect gives her cause of let, till she the towne have past:
And when she seeth the pleasant fields in safetie to have gayned,
Then joy therof all dread devoures, which erst her only payned.
What will ye more, th'appointed place at length she doth attayne,
Till Fortune please her love to send, there minding to remayne:
And whiles she doth the fountayn cleare, with thoughtful hope behold
And every let, her love may stay, unto her selfe unfolde.
A dreadfull Lyon downe desendes, from Mountaine huge therby,
With thundring pace, whose sodain sight, when Thisbie can espy:
No maruel was though terror then, and straungenes of the sight,
Within a simple maydens brest, all counsayle put to flight.
Howbeit, though counsayle fayld, yet feare so did that place possesse,
That as the tender brest, whose age no feare did yet oppresse:
Now seeth his foe, with ravening Jaw, him ready to receave,
Sets winges unto his littell legs, himselfe poore foole to save.
Even so this Mayd, her enemy flees, unto a hollow tree:
For succor flyes, whose ruthful mone, did succor not denye:
But close her keepes. The Liones fearce, that in the Mountayne wilde,
Devoured had, new slaughtred beastes, and empty belly filde:
With moossell all embrude with blood, drawes to the cristal Well,
He dranke, and in his backe returne, this fatall hap befell.
Amid this way a kercheife white, which frighted Thisbie had
Let fall by chaunce, as feare and haste, unto the tree her lad:
This Lion findes, and with his mouth, yet smoaking all in gore▪
And armid pawes it staynes with blood, and all in sunder tore.
That doone away he windes, as fier of Hell, or Vulcans thunder
Blew in his tayle, or as his corps it seas▪d to teare a sunder:
Now Pyramus who could not earst, the wrathfull house forgo,
Hath past the towne, and as he drew the Fountayn neare unto:
The cloth he spies, which when (alas) all stained so he saw,
In sunder tore, the ground about, full traste with Lyons paw:
The Silver streames with strekes of blood, besprent and troubled new,
And there again that cursed trace, the woful print to shew:
A sure beleefe did straight inuade, his overlyving minde,
T•at there the fatall ende (alas) of Thisbie was assinde:
And that her dainty flesh, of beastes a pray unmeet was made,
