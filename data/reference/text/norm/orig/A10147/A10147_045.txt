The slave of seruile sort, that borne is bond by kinde,
Doth not remayne in hope, with such unquiet minde:
Ne tossed crasid Ship, with yrksome surging seas,
So greedely the quiet Port, doth thirst to ride at ease.
As I thy short returne, with wishing vowes require,
In hope that of my hatefull harmes, the date will then expire:
But time with stealing steps, and driery dayes doth drive,
And thou remaynst then bound to come, if that thou be alive.
O cruell Tygars whelpe, who had thy hand in holde?
When the with flattering pen thou wrotst, thy help at hand behold?
Beleeve it to be true, I come without delay,
A foole and silly simple soule, yet doost thou still betray:
Whose mooveles love and trust, doth reason far surmount,
Whom Cupids trumpe, to fatall death hath sommond to accompt
My fayth and former life: fed with such frendly fier,
Have not of thee by just reward, deserved such falts hyer:
I promisse thee not mine, but thy case I bewayle,
What infamy may greater be, then of thy fayth to fayle?
How ofte with humble sute? have I besought the sonne,
That he would spur his Coursers fearce, their race more swifte to ronne?
To th'end with quicker speed, might come the promised day,
The day which I with loving lookes, and weary will did pray.
But thou art sure disposde to glory in my death,
Wherfore to feede thy fancy fond, loe, here I ende my breath.
I can not sighe nor sob, away by playnt I pine:
I see my fatall fainting file, ye Sisters do untwine,
The Feriman I finde, prest at the River side:
To take me in his restles Boate, therin with him to ride.
And yet although I sterue, through thy dispitous fault:
Yet crave I not in my revenge, that harme should thee assault,
But rather that thy fame, eternally may shine:
And that each to thine avayle, aboundantly encline.
That each thine enterprise, hath luckye lot and chaunce,
And stable fortune, thine estate, from day to day aduaunce,
That Sun, that Moone, that starres, and eke the plannets all,
The fier, the water, and the earth, may freendly to thee fal.
That many quiet yeres, thou number may with rest:
Uoyd of all annoyes and greeves, as may content thee best,
And if that foraine love, torment and vexe thy harte:
God yeeld thy weary wanting wish, and swagement of thy smart.
With froward flearing face, at me if Fortune frowne,
Thou doost reioyce and I not so, but joy thy good renowne:
And if I thee offend, for that I do thee love,
Forgive it me: for force it is, I can it not remove.
For I in secret sort, these lines to thee did I write,
My weakned wearied hand hensforth, shall sease for to endyte:
That letters to receive from me, thou neede not muse:
The messenger that next of all, of me shall bring the newes.
Dissolved from the corps, shalbe my dolefull spright:
That first (unshea•hd) shall passe to thee, when he hath vewd thy sight,
Contented he shall go unto the heavens above,
In case that joyed rested place, may gayne it any love.
And now for that my death, thy name may spot and stayne:
If that the flying fame therof, to others eares attayne,
I will not it were red, or knowen by other wayes:
That thou art only cause, I thus in ruthe do ende my dayes.
Wherfore this Letter red, condemne it to the flame:
And if thou do thy honnor forse, I know thou wilt thesame,
And if in lingring time, unwares they chaunce to come:
Wherin the entrayles of the earth, shall hap to be my tombe.
At least yet graunt me this, it is a small request:
O happy wythered pyned corps, God send thy soule good rest.
