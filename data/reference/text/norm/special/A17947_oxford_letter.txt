To my lovinge frende Thomas Bedingfeld Esquyer, one of her Maiesties gentlemen Pentioners.

After I had perused youre letters good maister Bedingfeld, findinge in then your request farre differing from the desert of your labour, I could not chose but greatly doubt, whether it were better for me to yelde you your desyre, or execute mine own intention towardes the publishinge of youre Booke. For I do confesse the affections that I have alwayes borne towardes you coulde move me not a little. But when I had throughlye considered in my mynde of sondrye and divers argumentes, whether it were best to obeye mine affections or the merites of your studyes. At the length I determined it better to denye your unlawfull request, then to graunte or condiscende to the concealment of so worthy a worke. Whereby as you have bene profited in the translatinge, so many may reape knowledge by the reading of the same, that shall comfort the afflicted, confirme the doubtful, encourage the cowarde, and lift up the base minded man, to atchiefe to any true summe or grade of vertue, wherto ought only the noble thoughtes of men to be enclyned. And because next to the sacred letters of Divinitye, nothinge doth perswade the same more then Philosophye, of which youre booke is plentifully stored. I thought my selfe to commit an unpardonable errour, to have murthered the same in the wast bottomes of my chestes, and better I thought it were to displease one, then to displease many: further consideringe so little a trifle cannot procure so great a breach of our amity, as may not with a little perswasions of reason be repayred agayne. And herein I am forced like a good and politicke Captaine, oftentimes to spoile and burne the corne of his own countrey, least his ennemyes therof do take aduauntage. For rather then so many of your countreye men shoulde be de•ided through my senister meanes of your industry in studyes, (wherof you are bound in conscience to yelde them an accompte) I am content to make spoyle and havocke of your request, and that that might have wrought greatly in me in this former respect, utterlye to be of no effect or operation, and when you examine your selfe what doth availe a masse of goulde to be continuallye imprisoned in your bags, and never to be employed to your use. I do not doubte even so you thinke of your studyes and delightfull Muses. What do they availe, if you do not participate tbem to others? Wherfore we have this latine Proverbe. Scire tuun nihil est nisi te scire hoc sciat alter. What doth availe the tree unlesse it yeld fruite unto an other, what doth availe the Vyne unlesse an other delighteth in the Grape? Uvhat doth availe the Rose unlesse an other toke pleasure in the smell? Whye should this tree be accompted better then that tree, but for the goodnes of his fruite? Whye should this Vyne be better then that Vyne, unlesse it brought forth a better Grape then the other? Whye should this Rose be better esteemed then that Rose, unlesse in pleasantnes of smel it farre surpassed the other Rose? And so is it in all other thinges as well as in man. Whye should this man, be more esteemed then that man, but for his vertue, throughe which everye man desireth to be accompted of. Then you amongest men I do not doubt, but will aspyre to followe that vertuous pathe, to illuster your selfe with the ornamentes of vertue. And in mine opynion as it beutifyeth a fayre woman to be decked with pearles and precious stones, so much more it ornifyeth a gentleman to be furnished in mynde with glittering vertues. Wherefore considering the small harme I do to you, the great good I do to others I prefer mine own intention to discover your volume, before your request to secrete the same: Wherein I may seeme to you to playe the part of the cunninge and experte Medeciner or Phisition, who althoughe his pacient in the extremitye of his burninge Fever, is desirous of colde liccour or drincke to qualefye his sore thirst, or rather kill his languishinge bodye. Yet for the daunger he doth evidentlye knowe by his science to ensue, denyeth hym the same. So you being sicke of to much doubte in your own procedinges, throughe which infirmitye you are desirous to burye and insevill your workes in the grave of oblivion. Yet I knowinge the discommodityes that shall redounde to your selfe thereby (and which is more unto your Countreyemen) as one that is willing to salve so great an inconuenience, am nothing dainty to denye your request. Againe we see, if our frendes be deade, we cannot shewe or declare our affection more then by erectinge them of Tombes: whereby when they be deade in deede, yet make we them live as it were againe through their monument, but with me behold it happeneth farre better, for in your lyfe time I shall erect you such a monument, that as I saye in your life time you shall see howe noble a shadowe of your vertuous life, shall hereafter remaine when you are deade and gone. And in your life time againe I say, I shall give you that monument and remembraunce of your lyfe, whereby I may declare my good will thoughe with your ill will as yet that I do beare you in your life. Thus earnestlye desyringe you in this one request of mine, as I would yelde to you in a great manye, not to repugne the settinge forth of your own proper studyes. I bid you farewel.

¶From my newe countrye Muses at Wivenghole, wishing you as you have begunne, to proceede in these vertuous actions. For when all things shall els forsake us, vertue yet will ever abide with us, and when our bodies falles into the bowels of the earth, yet that shall mounte with our mindes into the highest Heavens.

By your lovinge and assured frende. E. Oxenford.
